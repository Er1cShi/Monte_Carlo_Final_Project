1    Project Scope
Our project studies how Multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) can be used to improve the compu-
tational efficiency of option pricing. The main motivation is that standard Monte Carlo (MC)
simulation is flexible and easy to apply, but it can become computationally expensive when high
accuracy is required, especially for path-dependent options. We aim to begin from the classical
Black–Scholes setting, reproduce baseline results using standard Monte Carlo, and then move
to MLMC for both European and path-dependent options.
    The project will proceed in five stages. First, we will study the Black–Scholes model and
standard Monte Carlo pricing, and reproduce benchmark results for European options. Second,
we will learn and implement the MLMC algorithm for European option pricing. Third, we
will extend the MLMC framework to a path-dependent option, most likely an arithmetic Asian
option. Fourth, we will propose a modification to the original MLMC sample allocation rule by
introducing an adaptive allocation strategy based on iterative variance estimation across levels.
Finally, we will compare the modified MLMC algorithm with the original MLMC in terms of
pricing accuracy, variance, root mean squared error, and computational cost.
    Our project is designed to match the three required elements of the course project. The
learning component is the study of Black–Scholes pricing, standard Monte Carlo simulation, and
the MLMC algorithm. The analysis component is the empirical comparison of standard MC,
original MLMC, and modified MLMC across different option types and parameter settings. The
research component is the modification of the MLMC sample allocation rule and the evaluation
of whether this modification improves efficiency in practice.


2    Background and Motivation
Under the Black–Scholes framework, the underlying asset price follows geometric Brownian
motion. For a European call option, the arbitrage-free price has a closed-form expression,
which makes it an ideal benchmark for validating simulation-based pricing methods. In standard
Monte Carlo pricing, the option value is estimated by simulating many asset paths and averaging
discounted payoffs. However, for a target root mean squared error of order ε, standard Monte
Carlo with Euler discretization can have computational cost of order O(ε−3 ).
    MLMC reduces this cost by combining estimators across multiple discretization levels. In-
stead of simulating only on the finest grid, MLMC writes the finest-level expectation as a

The idea is that coarse levels are cheap and capture most of the expectation, while fine levels
estimate small corrections with relatively low variance if the fine and coarse paths are properly
coupled. This makes MLMC especially attractive for option pricing problems in which standard
Monte Carlo becomes too costly.


3     Methods
3.1     Stage 1: Black–Scholes and Standard Monte Carlo
We will first implement the Black–Scholes formula for European call options under geometric
Brownian motion and compare it against standard Monte Carlo simulation. This will provide:

    • a benchmark for validating simulation code,

    • a reference point for pricing accuracy,

    • and a baseline for later computational comparisons.

3.2     Stage 2: MLMC for European Options
Next, we will implement MLMC for European option pricing. We will construct coupled fine
and coarse paths using the same Brownian path, estimate correction terms Pbℓ − Pbℓ−1 , and
allocate samples across levels according to the standard MLMC sample allocation rule. We will
compare standard MC and MLMC in terms of:

    • estimated option values,

    • variance,

    • root mean squared error (RMSE),

    • and computational cost.

3.3     Stage 3: Extension to a Path-Dependent Option
We will then extend the MLMC framework to a path-dependent option, most likely an arithmetic
Asian option. This is a natural next step because MLMC becomes more meaningful when no
simple closed-form benchmark exists. For the Asian option, the payoff depends on the average
price along the path rather than only the terminal price, making the simulation problem more
challenging and more representative of realistic applications.

3.4   Stage 4: Modification of the Sample Allocation Rule
The main research contribution of our project will be a modification of the MLMC sample
allocation strategy. In the original algorithm, the number of samples on each level depends
on estimated level variances and costs. Our proposed modification is to make this allocation
more adaptive by updating the sample sizes iteratively as improved variance estimates become
available. Intuitively, this may reduce unnecessary sampling on levels whose correction variance
is already stable or negligible.
    At a high level, our modified approach will:

 1. start with a pilot sample on each level,

 2. estimate level variances,

 3. allocate additional samples according to current variance estimates,

 4. update the variance estimates again,

 5. and repeat until the target accuracy is reached.

3.5   Stage 5: Comparison and Evaluation
Finally, we will compare:

  • standard Monte Carlo,

  • original MLMC,

  • and modified MLMC.

   The main evaluation metrics will include:

  • pricing error,

  • standard error,

  • RMSE,

  • runtime,

  • total computational cost,

  • and level-by-level sample allocation.

   Our key research question is:

      Can an adaptive sample allocation strategy improve the practical efficiency of MLMC
      for option pricing compared with the original allocation rule?

4     Expected Outcomes
We expect the following outcomes:

    • Standard MLMC should outperform standard Monte Carlo in computational efficiency for
      a fixed target accuracy.

    • The performance gain should be more visible for path-dependent options than for simple
      European options.

    • Our adaptive allocation modification may reduce computational cost further by distributing
      sampling effort more effectively across levels.

   We also recognize that the modified allocation may not always outperform the original
method. If so, that would still be a meaningful result, since part of the project is to test
whether a plausible algorithmic change actually improves performance.
